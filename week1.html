What is Number Theory?

Number theory is the study of integers (represented as $\mathbb{Z}$).

Topics we will cover include:

  • Divisors and divisibility
  • gcd, lcm, and the Euclidean algorithm
  • Number bases (base 2, base 10, etc.)
  • Modular arithmetic
    • Fermat's little theorem
    • Euler's theorem
    • Chinese remainder theorem
  • Diophantine equations

Divisors

A divisor of an integer is a number that evenly divides that integer.

For example, the divisors of 6 are 1, 2, 3, and 6.

The formal definition is as follows:

Definition of Divisor
An integer $d$ is a divisor of integer $n$ if there exists an integer $k$ such that $n = kd$.

We may also write $d \mid n$ (read "d divides n") to show that $d$ is a divisor of $n$.

Divisibility

An integer $n$ is divisible by $d$ if $d$ is a divisor of $n$.

\[d \mid n \Leftrightarrow \text{$n$ is divisible by $d$} \]

We can also say that $n$ is a multiple of $d$.

Is $30$ divisible by $2$? $30 = 15 \cdot 2$

Is $30$ divisible by $3$? $30 = 10 \cdot 3$

Is $30$ divisible by $4$? No

Divisibility

Some useful facts about divisibility:

  • $a \mid a$ for all $a$.
  • If $a \mid b$ and $b \mid c$ then $a \mid c$.
  • $1 \mid a$ and $a \mid 0$ for all $a$.
  • If $a \mid b$ then $a \mid bc$ for any $c$.
  • If $a \mid b$ and $b \mid a$ then $a = \pm b$.
  • If $a \mid b$ and $a \mid c$ then $a \mid (mb + nc)$ for all $m$, $n$.

Divisibility Rules

A number is divisible:

  • by 2 if its last digit is even.
  • by 3 if the sum of its digits is divisible by 3.
  • by 4 if the number formed by its last two digits is divisible by 4.
  • by 5 if its last digit is 0 or 5.
  • by 6 if it is divisible by both 2 and 3.
  • by 7 if twice the last digit minus the number formed by the remaining digits is divisible by 7.
  • by 8 if the number formed by its last three digits is divisible by 8.
  • by 9 if the sum of its digits is divisible by 9.
  • by 10 if its last digit is 0.
  • by 11 if the difference between the sum of the digits in odd positions and the sum of the digits in even positions is divisible by 11.

Greatest Common Divisor

Two numbers can share divisors! For example, $12$ and $18$ are both divisible by $1$, $2$, $3$, and $6$.

Greatest Common Divisor
The greatest common divisor (gcd) of two integers $a$ and $b$ is the largest integer that divides both $a$ and $b$. We denote this as $\gcd(a, b)$.

What is $\gcd(12, 18)$? $\gcd(12, 18) = 6$

What is $\gcd(12, 11)$? $\gcd(12, 11) = 1$

If $\gcd(a, b) = 1$, then $a$ and $b$ are coprime.

Least Common Multiple

Two numbers can also share multiples! For example, $12$ and $18$ both divide $36$, $72$, $108$, and so on.

Least Common Multiple
The least common multiple (lcm) of two integers $a$ and $b$ is the smallest number that can be evenly divided by both $a$ and $b$. We denote this as $\lcm(a, b)$.

What is $\lcm(12, 18)$? $\lcm(12, 18) = 36$

What is $\lcm(12, 11)$? $\lcm(12, 11) = 132$

Prime Numbers

Remember 7? Its only positive divisors were $1$ and $7$.

A prime number is a natural number greater than 1 with exactly two distinct positive divisors: 1 and itself.

The Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic
Every integer greater than 1 is either prime or can be uniquely written as a product of prime numbers.

For example, we can write $12 = 4 \cdot 3 = 2 \cdot 2 \cdot 3 = 2^2 \cdot 3$.

We say the prime factorization of $12$ is $2^2 \cdot 3$.

Note: 1 is neither prime nor composite!

Number of divisors

If the prime factorization of $n$ is \[ n = p_1^{e_1} p_2^{e_2} \cdots p_k^{e_k}, \] then each divisor of $n$ looks like \[ p_1^{b_1} p_2^{b_2} \cdots p_k^{b_k} \]

where each exponent $b_i$ ranges from $0$ to $e_i$.

To count the number of divisors, we multiply the number of choices for each exponent: $\boxed{(e_1 + 1)(e_2 + 1) \cdots (e_k + 1)}$.

Sum of divisors

We can do something similar for the sum of divisors.

Let $n$ prime factorize as

\[ n = p_1^{e_1} p_2^{e_2} \cdots p_k^{e_k}. \]

To sum across all possible divisors, sum across all possible exponents for each prime factor, then multiply the sums together:

\[ \boxed{(1 + p_1 + p_1^2 + \cdots + p_1^{e_1})(1 + p_2 + p_2^2 + \cdots + p_2^{e_2}) \cdots (1 + p_k + p_k^2 + \cdots + p_k^{e_k})} \]

Computing GCD and LCM

Prime factorization can help us quickly compute the GCD and LCM. Suppose $a = 2^3 \cdot 3^2 $ and $b = 2^2 \cdot 3^4$.

The GCD must divide both $a$ and $b$, so for each prime, you can only use as many copies as the number with fewer copies has.

$\gcd(a, b) = 2^{\min(3, 2)} \cdot 3^{\min(2, 4)} = 2^2 \cdot 3^2$

The LCM must be divisible by both $a$ and $b$, so it needs enough copies of each prime to cover both numbers.

$\lcm(a, b) = 2^{\max(3, 2)} \cdot 3^{\max(2, 4)} = 2^3 \cdot 3^4$

Division

Division is not formally defined in the integers, since the result of dividing two integers is not always an integer.

However, we can formalize the concept of quotient and remainder:

Euclidean Division
Given two integers $a$ and $b$ with $b \neq 0$, there exist unique integers $q$ and $r$ such that \[ a = bq + r \] and $0 \leq r < |b|$. We call $a$ the dividend, $b$ the divisor, $q$ the quotient, and $r$ the remainder.

For example, we can "divide" 9 by 4 to get $9 = 4(2) + 1$.

The Euclidean Algorithm

Computing the GCD using prime factorization is very hard for large numbers.

The Euclidean Algorithm is a highly efficient method for computing the GCD using only repeated subtraction (or division).

To compute $\gcd(a, b)$:

  1. Suppose $a > b$. Subtract $b$ from $a$ until the result is less than $b$ (but not negative). Let the result be $r$.
  2. We have $\gcd(a, b) = \gcd(b, r)$. Repeat steps 1 and 2 until one of the numbers becomes 0.
  3. The other number is the GCD.

Example

Suppose we want to compute $\gcd(1071, 462)$. Using the Euclidean Algorithm:

  1. $\gcd(\textcolor{red}{1071}, \textcolor{blue}{462}) = \gcd(\textcolor{red}{147}, \textcolor{blue}{462})$
  2. $\gcd(\textcolor{blue}{147}, \textcolor{red}{462}) = \gcd(\textcolor{blue}{147}, \textcolor{red}{21})$
  3. $\gcd(\textcolor{red}{147}, \textcolor{blue}{21}) = \gcd(\textcolor{red}{0}, \textcolor{blue}{21})$

Thus, $\gcd(1071, 462) = \boxed{21}$.

Proving the Euclidean Algorithm

Write $a = bq + r$ with $0 \leq r < |b|$. Then $r = a - bq$.

Suppose a divisor $d$ satisfies $d \mid a$ and $d \mid b$. Then $d \mid (a - bq) = r$ (integer combination). This shows that any common divisor of $a$ and $b$ is also a divisor of $b$ and $r$.

Similarly, suppose a divisor $d$ satisfies $d \mid b$ and $d \mid r$. Then $d \mid (bq + r) = a$. This shows that any common divisor of $b$ and $r$ is also a divisor of $a$ and $b$.

Since this argument work for any divisor $d$, the sets of common divisors of $(a, b)$ and $(b, r)$ are the same.

If the sets are the same, their greatest elements (GCD) must also be the same. This proves $\gcd(a, b) = \gcd(b, r)$.
